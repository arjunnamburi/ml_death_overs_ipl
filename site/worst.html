Worst-Predicted Balls

The 2026 death balls XGBoost got most wrong, each with a hypothesis for why. Open a ball with Show why to see what pushed XGBoost away from the real outcome.

How to read this page
  • Three lists (the tabs): the ten balls with XGBoost’s highest log-loss; the ten where XGBoost lost most to the scorecard baseline (B1); and the worst three of each outcome. A ball can be in more than one list.
  • The small table gives the chance each model put on what actually happened, and the log-loss that follows from it. The lower the chance, the higher the log-loss.
  • The tag says whose miss it was. Rare for every model: even B1 gave it under 1%. XGBoost’s own miss: B1 gave it at least twice XGBoost’s chance. Shared miss: everything else.
  • The hypothesis is one of five patterns, explained below the lists. Show why gives the full reasoning for that ball.
  • The push bars (inside Show why) show which features moved XGBoost toward what happened (right) or away from it (left), compared with its guess for an average ball. As a rough guide (approximately, and for less likely outcomes), −0.3 cuts the chance by about a quarter, −0.7 about halves it and −1.4 cuts it to about a quarter. Hover a bar for the exact figure.
  • Pushes are the model’s reasons, not causes on the field.

The ten 2026 death balls with XGBoost’s highest log-loss: the outcomes it thought least likely.

Why so many threes? 7 of these 10 balls are threes. Threes are so rare that every model gives them a tiny chance, and a tiny chance means a large log-loss when one happens. Balls tagged Rare for every model are hard for any model, not only XGBoost; the other two tabs show more of XGBoost’s own misses.

Five Hypotheses, Shared Across the Balls

Each hypothesis was written from a handful of balls, then checked on all 3,583 death balls of 2026 (notebook). Three of the five (B, C and E) don’t show up as a pattern across the season, so the balls behind them are more likely rare outcomes than blind spots in the model. The other two, threes being rare for every model (A) and wickets by chance (D), match the season.

AThrees are rare for every model

Shared by 7 balls: AR Patel (3), Himmat Singh (3), Nithish Kumar Reddy (3), AS Roy (3), JM Sharma (3), AJ Hosein (3), N Burger (3)

Seven threes. Threes are about 1 in 500 death balls (7 of 3,583 in 2026), so every model gave each of these under about 0.35%. For most of them XGBoost's batter-history and ground features pushed further against a three (a frequent six-hitter, a ground where many balls go for boundaries), but the difference from B1 is small in absolute terms. Our hypothesis: these are rare events, the price of a model that is right on average, and some threes come from overthrows or misfields that no feature can see.

Checked on all 2026 death balls: across all 2026 death balls, does XGBoost predict threes about as often as they happen?

Matches the season. Threes were 0.20% of 2026 death balls, and XGBoost predicted 0.16% (gap +0.03 points, 95% interval −0.11 to +0.21). With only seven threes the check can't rule out a small miss, but there is no sign of a blind spot.

BA new batter is expected to play himself in

Shared by 6 balls: Kartik Tyagi (6), TA Boult (6), XC Bartlett (6), Shashank Singh (6), K Rabada (6), Washington Sundar (6)

Sixes hit by batters who had only just come in. Having no runs or balls yet, for the batter and for the partnership, pushed hardest against the six. Our hypothesis: in earlier seasons a new batter usually took a ball or two before attacking, while in 2026 more of them attacked at once. When the new batter was also a tailender (Kartik Tyagi, TA Boult, XC Bartlett, K Rabada), his low strike rate at the death pushed against the six as well, so the model was doubly sure.

Checked on all 2026 death balls: across the 2026 death, do batters in their first three balls, and batters at 8-11, hit more sixes than XGBoost predicts?

Not seen across the season. Batters in their first three balls hit sixes about as often as XGBoost predicted (5.2% against 5.5%), and so did new batters at No. 8-11 (4.9% against 4.4%). So these sixes are more likely rare events than a blind spot.

CThe last balls of an innings are read as an all-out slog

Shared by 10 balls: Vishnu Vinod (0), DL Chahar (0), Ramandeep Singh (0), H Klaasen (0), C Green (1), MD Choudhary (1), C Bosch (1), SR Dubey (4), CV Varun (4), M Prasidh Krishna (4)

The final balls of an innings, often with nine down. The scorecard features dominate: few balls left per wicket, the ball count and the batting positions. XGBoost read these balls as all-out slogs and gave a dot far less chance than B1. Our hypothesis: the finer scorecard that brings most of XGBoost's gain also makes it overconfident here, where batters still miss, defend or take the single.

Checked on all 2026 death balls: does XGBoost under-predict dots and singles across the 20th over of 2026 (not just on these balls)? If so, this also explains part of the season-wide under-prediction of death dots.

Not seen across the season. In the 20th over, dots and singles came about as often as XGBoost predicted. Dots are under-predicted in overs 16-19 as well, so the last over doesn't seem special: it looks like part of the death-wide dot gap in Figure 6 of the report.

DWickets with no clear cause

Shared by 3 balls: RK Singh (wicket), TH David (wicket), RM Patidar (wicket)

Three wickets early in the death, with wickets in hand. No feature pushed hard. XGBoost gives such balls a lower wicket chance than B1, and across the season that lower chance is about right (Figure 6 in the report). So we read these as chance, the cost of being right on average, not as a feature that misled the model.

Checked on all 2026 death balls: early in the death with wickets in hand, is XGBoost's wicket chance about right?

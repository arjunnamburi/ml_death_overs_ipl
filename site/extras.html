Data Checks

The checks behind the quality issues listed in the report’s Data section.

  • The 64 raw columns, in groups: the match (id, date, season, venue, teams, toss, result and method); the ball (innings, over and ball number); the players (batter, non-striker and bowler); the runs (off the bat, extras with their type, and the total); the dismissal (kind, player out and fielders); and running totals for the team, batter and bowler.
  • On the raw file (review_checks notebook): no fully identical rows; 807 distinct player names, none differing only by case, spaces or dots; 4 of 47,756 overs with seven legal balls in the source data, whose 7th balls were dropped when cleaning; 175 super-over rows, also dropped; and, among the 1,243 matches, 16 ties, 9 no-results and 23 with a revised target (for example, by D/L). Missing values are confined to columns that are empty by design, such as reviews, fielders, dismissal kind and the target in the first innings.
  • Wides and no-balls: the model tells them apart by whether the ball counts as faced (a no-ball does, a wide doesn’t), not by the raw extra_type column, which was dropped when cleaning. Checked against that column, the split matches on all 11,092 extras: 9,871 wides and 1,221 no-balls. Wides never have runs off the bat.
  • Free hits are built forward from no-balls and carry over through a wide or another no-ball: 1,163 legal free hits in all, 60 in 2026 and 21 at the 2026 death. From 2016, when every no-ball gave a free hit, there are no bowler wickets on 666 free hits (2 run-outs and 1 retired hurt). Before 2016, when only some no-balls gave a free hit, there were 8 bowler wickets on the 497 balls marked as free hits, so some of those weren’t real free hits.

Source: notebooks/models.ipynb (the recency-weighting and death-only cells)

  • Why recent-season weights didn’t help: down-weighting old seasons leaves the model less data: the best number of trees fell from 340 at full weight to 92 at a half-life of one season, and sixes on 2024 ended up more under-predicted, not less (−0.58 points at full weight, −0.87 at a half-life of one season). The season features (league run rate this season, impact-player era) let the model tell seasons apart, but they can’t carry a trend forward, because trees can’t extrapolate: a tree’s output is constant beyond its last split. The highest split on the season run rate is 9.25 runs an over, so a season run rate above that gets the same treatment as the highest values seen in training. The logistic regression can extrapolate, but only in a straight line.

Why These Two Models

  • Logistic regression adds up the effect of each feature. It is the strong simple model, so its gap to XGBoost shows what curves and interactions add.
  • XGBoost builds boosted trees, which find interactions on their own (for example, wickets in hand matter more at the death). It optimises log-loss directly and handles missing values and categories without extra steps.
  • Depth 4 with large leaves won a small grid on 2024. All six settings scored within 0.0014 of each other, so the result doesn’t hang on lucky tuning. Deeper trees with small leaves overfit.
  • One XGBoost model for all overs was kept, because the death-only model was no better on 2025 (table above).

Which Players the Model Reads Best and Worst (2026)

How to read the player, match and ablation tables
  • Gain vs B1 is XGBoost’s log-loss minus the scorecard baseline’s, per ball, on that player’s or match’s balls. Negative means XGBoost read them better. The brackets are 95% intervals from resampling matches.
  • “vs expected” compares what happened with what XGBoost expected on the same balls: runs, strike rate (runs per 100 balls), sixes and wickets. Positive means more than expected.
  • Ablation tables show how much worse XGBoost got when a group of features was removed and the model refit. Positive means the group was helping.

44 batters (at least 150 balls faced) and 61 bowlers (at least 120 balls bowled) in 2026, all overs. XGBoost reads 24 of the batters and 32 of the bowlers clearly better than B1, and no player clearly worse.

  • The best-read batters are big hitters, such as Suryavanshi, Allen and Abhishek Sharma. Their history most likely tells XGBoost they hit sixes, which B1 can’t know.
  • The worst-read batters had slower seasons than their history suggested, such as Jaiswal and Washington Sundar.
  • For bowlers it runs the other way: the worst-read conceded far fewer runs than expected, such as Ngidi and Bhuvneshwar Kumar. The model barely tells bowlers apart.
  • No player, batter or bowler, is read clearly worse than by B1.

What the Errors Say

  • The worst balls are rare outcomes, such as threes, sixes from new batters and dots on the final balls. Checked across the season, none of the patterns behind them showed up as a blind spot.
  • The one systematic miss is too few dots at the death. A calibration step fitted on death balls alone could fix it.
  • Free hits could be handled by a rule rather than learned: only a run-out can dismiss the batter, and there are too few free hits for the model to learn that.

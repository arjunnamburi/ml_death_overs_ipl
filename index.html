1. Research Question

Using only information available before each delivery, how well can the probabilities of the seven outcomes of a death-over IPL delivery (0, 1, 2, 3, 4 or 6 runs, or a wicket) be predicted for an unseen season (2026), and how much of that comes from detailed context (batter, bowler, matchup history, momentum, chase pressure) beyond the basic match situation of phase, wickets fallen and innings?

The last five overs decide most T20 matches, and they are the hardest to predict: the seven outcomes are spread more evenly there than in any other phase (Figure 2). A probability for each ball is the building block for questions such as who should bowl the 19th over. “How well” is measured by log-loss, which rewards giving the real outcome a high chance. The “basic match situation” is the scorecard baseline (B1), which knows only the phase, the wickets band and the innings.

The answer in short:

  • How well: XGBoost beats the basic match situation (B1) on 2026 death balls by 2.1%.
  • Where it comes from: the largest part comes from reading the scorecard more finely (exact balls and wickets left, run rate, totals). B1 can’t do this: it can’t tell over 16 from over 20. Averaged over every order of adding the four kinds of information (Shapley shares), the finer scorecard gives about half of the gain at the death (53%) and in-match context about a third (35%).
  • The detailed context: against a stronger scorecard baseline (B1+, XGBoost on the 17 scorecard features only, added after the test), the full model is only 0.4% better at the death, and that can’t be told from zero. Within it, in-match context (momentum, chase pressure, partnership, the bowler’s spell) helps clearly in every order it is added. Player type and batter, bowler and matchup history can’t be told from zero at the death, although history is the largest part on all balls (38%).

Checks on the data:

  • Retired hurt is not counted as a wicket, and every missing value left in the feature table is intentional (for example, no target in the first innings).

Checked against that column, the split matches on all 11,092 extras: 9,871 wides and 1,221 no-balls. Wides never have runs off the bat.
  • Free hits are built forward from no-balls and carry over through a wide or another no-ball: 1,163 legal free hits in all, 60 in 2026 and 21 at the 2026 death. From 2016, when every no-ball gave a free hit, there are no bowler wickets on 666 free hits (2 run-outs and 1 retired hurt). Before 2016, when only some no-balls gave a free hit, there were 8 bowler wickets on the 497 balls marked as free hits, so some of those weren’t real free hits.
How it works: Why the test is a later season, not a random sample
  • What: A time-based split trains on earlier seasons and tests on a later one. A random split would mix balls from every season into both sets.
  • Why here: The game drifts (Figure 1): sixes nearly doubled from 2008 to 2026. A random split would let the model see 2026-style balls in training, so the test would look better than real use, where the model always predicts a season it hasn’t seen. Splitting by season also keeps every match whole, because balls in one match share a pitch, teams and conditions.
  • The choice: Train on 2008-2024, choose between models on 2025 (validation), and score 2026 once. Choosing on a separate season keeps the test untouched: any choice made by looking at a set makes that set’s score optimistic.
  • What it showed: The drift still matters. Trained up to 2025, XGBoost still under-predicts 2026 dots at the death, and calibration learned on one season didn’t carry over to the next (see the calibration box in section 4).
How it works: Entropy: how hard a phase is to guess
  • What: For outcome shares p1 … p7, entropy is H = −Σ pk ln pk. It is 0 when one outcome always happens and largest, ln 7 ≈ 1.95, when all seven are equally likely.
  • Link to log-loss: A model that knows only a phase’s true outcome shares, and gives every ball in the phase those shares, scores a log-loss equal to the entropy. So entropy is the score of “knowing the phase and nothing else”.
  • What it showed: The death is the most even phase (1.60), so it is the hardest to guess. B1 scores 1.6017 at the 2026 death, about that level. XGBoost’s 1.5680 is below it, so the ball-level features tell it something beyond the phase.

3. Preprocessing

  • The label is one of seven outcomes on a legal ball: 0, 1, 2, 3, 4, 6 or W. It is the ball’s total runs, because that is what changes the score, so byes and leg-byes count (5 runs count as 4, and 7 as 6). B1 uses the same label.
  • Wickets override runs: a run-out after a completed run is labelled W. Retired hurt is not a wicket.
  • The death is every legal ball after the first 90 legal balls of an innings, which is from over 16.
  • Features are built only from earlier balls, so nothing about the ball being predicted leaks in.
  • History features (batter, bowler, matchup and venue) use a three-season window: the two previous seasons plus the current season before the match. Each rate is pulled toward a group average, so a player with few balls stays close to it: (balls × own rate + 100 × group rate) / (balls + 100). Batters are pulled toward their batting-position group (top 1-3, middle 4-5, finisher 6-8, tail 9-11), bowlers toward their bowling type in that phase, and venues toward the league. The strength of 100 balls was fixed, the same as in B1, rather than tuned, so that 2025 was kept for comparing the models.
  • For the logistic regression, the 87 features become 118 inputs: one column per category for the 5 categorical features, flags for missing values, clipping and standardising. The flags, clipping limits and scaling are learned on the training seasons only, and re-learned on 2008-2025 for the refit.
  • The impact-player rule (from 2023) is a feature, impact_player_era, in the Venue and season group. In 2026 it is 1 on every ball, so it only tells the model which era a training ball came from. It ranks 38th of 87 by average push at the 2026 death.
  • No recent-form features (a player’s last few matches) were built: such short windows hold too few balls and are too noisy, so the three-season history stands in for form.
How it works: Leakage: only information from before the ball
  • What: Leakage is a feature that holds information not known when the prediction is made. The classic case is a running total that already includes the ball being predicted.
  • Why it matters: A leaked feature makes a model look far better on the test than it could ever be in use. In ball-by-ball data it hides easily: the raw file’s running totals for the team, batter and bowler are taken after the ball.
  • The choice: Every in-match feature is built from earlier balls only, so running totals are shifted back one ball. History windows use the two previous seasons plus the current season before this match, never the match itself.
  • How it was checked: The red flags were a score better than plausible, or a richer model doing worse than a simpler one. Neither appeared. After a double-counted delivery was found (section 2), the running totals were rebuilt from the balls themselves rather than taken from the raw file.
How it works: Shrinkage: trusting a rate only as much as its sample allows
  • What: A rate from a few balls is noisy: 2 sixes in 10 balls is a 20% six rate that means little. Shrinkage blends a player’s own rate with a group rate: (balls × own + k × group) / (balls + k), with k = 100.
  • How to read k: k acts like 100 imaginary balls at the group rate. The weight on the player’s own rate is balls / (balls + 100): 17% with 20 balls, 50% with 100 and 80% with 400.
  • Why: It is the bias-variance trade-off. The raw rate is unbiased but has a huge variance for new players. The group rate has a small variance but ignores the player. The blend beats both. This is the empirical-Bayes idea: the group average is the prior, and the player’s balls update it.
  • The choice: Batters shrink toward their batting-position group, bowlers toward their bowling type in that phase, and venues toward the league. k was fixed at 100, as in B1, rather than tuned, so that 2025 stayed free for comparing the models.

How it works: Why baselines first
  • What: A baseline is the simplest model a claim has to beat. B0 gives every ball the outcome shares of the training seasons. B1 gives each cell (phase × wickets band × innings) its own shares, shrunk toward B0 with the same k = 100.
  • Why: A log-loss of 1.568 means nothing on its own. It only means something next to what a simple model scores. B1 is the “basic match situation” of the research question, so XGBoost minus B1 is the question’s answer.
  • What it showed: B1 already gains 0.0417 over B0 at the death, more than XGBoost then adds on top (0.0338). Much of what can be predicted about a death ball is the match situation. B1+ (XGBoost on the scorecard only) measures what a finer reading of that situation is worth.
How it works: Multinomial logistic regression
  • What: For each outcome k the model computes a score zk = wk · x + bk, a weighted sum of the inputs. Softmax turns the seven scores into probabilities: pk = ezk / Σj ezj. The weights are fitted by minimising log-loss on the training balls, plus an L2 penalty that keeps them small (C = 1; a smaller C means a stronger penalty).
  • What it can’t do: Each feature moves the score by the same amount everywhere. “Wickets in hand matter more at the death” is an interaction, and the model can’t learn it unless such a feature is built by hand.
  • Why the preprocessing:
    • Categories become one-hot columns, because a weight needs a number.
    • Missing values get a flag column plus a fill value, because the model can’t take a blank.
    • Extreme values are clipped at the training 0.5th and 99.5th percentiles, so a few outliers don’t swing a weight.
    • Inputs are standardised, so the L2 penalty treats every feature on the same scale and the solver (L-BFGS) converges.
    • All of these are learned on the training seasons only; learning them on the test season would be a leak.
  • Its role: The strong simple model. Its gap to XGBoost is what curves and interactions add: −0.0084 [−0.0143, −0.0029] at the 2026 death.
How it works: Gradient-boosted trees (XGBoost)
  • What: Boosting builds many small trees in sequence, each correcting what the trees before it got wrong. With seven outcomes, each round adds one tree per outcome. Each tree is fitted to the gradient of log-loss (predicted probability minus 1 if that outcome happened, else minus 0), using the curvature too. Its output, times the learning rate, is added to that outcome’s score, and softmax turns the scores into probabilities, as in the logistic regression.
  • What each setting controls:
    • Depth 4: a tree can combine up to four features, so it learns interactions such as balls left × wickets in hand × required rate.
    • Minimum child weight 100: a leaf needs a total curvature of 100, which is a few hundred balls for common outcomes and far more for rare ones like threes, so leaves stay stable.
    • Learning rate 0.05: each tree takes a small step, which needs more trees but overfits less.
    • Row and column sampling 0.8: each tree sees a random 80% of balls and features, which adds variety and reduces overfitting.
    • Early stopping: trees are added until the held-out loss hasn’t improved for 50 rounds. The final model has 301 rounds, that is 301 × 7 trees.
  • The choice: A first fit at depth 6 with minimum child weight 1 overfit: its training loss was 0.115 below its 2024 loss, and it stopped after 135 rounds. The grid on 2024 then moved to shallower trees and bigger leaves (depth 3, 4, 6 × minimum child weight 20, 100). All six settings scored within 0.0014 of each other, bigger leaves were better at every depth, and depth 4 with 100 won. Missing values need no flags: each split learns which way to send them.
  • What it showed: It beats the logistic regression most at the death, where interactions matter most: −0.0084 there against −0.0026 on all balls.

How it works: Calibration: temperature vs vector scaling
  • What: A model is calibrated when its 10% predictions happen about 10% of the time. The reliability diagram (Figure 6) checks this by grouping balls by predicted chance. Calibration is separate from discrimination, which is telling risky balls from safe ones: a model that gives every ball the season shares is calibrated but useless. Log-loss rewards both.
  • The two fixes, both fitted on held-out predictions, never on the training balls:
    • Temperature scaling: pk = softmax(zk / T). One number: T > 1 softens over-confident predictions and T < 1 sharpens them, but it can’t fix one outcome being too low overall.
    • Vector scaling: pk = softmax(ak zk + bk). Fourteen numbers, a scale and a shift per outcome, so it can also fix “too few dots, too many wickets”.
  • The choice: Each fix was learned on one season and judged on the next (2025). Vector scaling helped the logistic regression, whose errors are systematic because it is linear. It hurt XGBoost (+0.0019 on all balls, +0.0022 at the death), even though it had helped on the season it was learned on (2024): the correction fitted that season’s shift, which didn’t carry over. So XGBoost stays raw.
  • What it showed: XGBoost is close to calibrated on all balls, but at the 2026 death it under-predicts dots (2.23 points) and over-predicts wickets (1.01 points). A fix learned on earlier seasons couldn’t have known about that.

Why These Two Models

  • Logistic regression adds up the effect of each feature. It is the strong simple model, so its gap to XGBoost shows what curves and interactions add.
  • XGBoost builds boosted trees, which find interactions on their own (for example, wickets in hand matter more at the death). It optimises log-loss directly and handles missing values and categories without extra steps.
  • Depth 4 with large leaves won a small grid on 2024. All six settings scored within 0.0014 of each other, so the result doesn’t hang on lucky tuning. Deeper trees with small leaves overfit.
  • One XGBoost model for all overs was kept, because the death-only model was no better on 2025 (table above).
How it works: Why one model for all overs
  • What: A model trained on death balls only can learn death-specific patterns, but from about 4.4 times fewer balls. A model trained on all overs has more data and can still tell the death apart through features such as balls left.
  • The rule: A death-only model is used only if its 2025 death-ball interval against the all-overs model is entirely below zero.
  • What it showed: For XGBoost, death-only minus all-overs was +0.0004 [−0.0031, +0.0040], so the all-overs model was kept: depth-4 trees already split on the phase where it matters.

5. Evaluation

  • Log-loss (the headline) is the average of −log(the probability given to what actually happened). Lower is better. A model that gives the real outcome a 20% chance scores 1.61 on that ball.
  • Brier score (a check) is the squared distance between the seven probabilities and what happened. It agrees with log-loss on every comparison here.
  • Two models are always compared on the same balls, and the difference is what’s reported.
How to read the intervals A difference is written as −0.0338 [−0.0468, −0.0212]. The first number is the difference on 2026. The two numbers in brackets are a 95% interval: the range the difference would likely fall in if 2026 had been played with a different set of matches. It comes from resampling the 71 matches 2,000 times, whole matches at a time, because balls in one match aren’t independent.
  • If the whole interval is below zero, the first model is better, and chance is an unlikely explanation.
  • If the interval crosses zero, we can’t tell the two apart on this data.
  • When many comparisons are made at once, a stricter version of the interval is used (Bonferroni). The paired table has 8 planned tests (4 comparisons on 2 sets of balls), so its stricter interval is about 1.4 times as wide (z ≈ 2.73 instead of 1.96). The tables say when an interval also clears that.
  • What the tests can show: that one model gave better probabilities than another on 2026 death balls, and by how much.
  • What they can’t show: why it was better, whether it will hold in other seasons, or anything about cause and effect on the field. With 3,583 death balls, the death intervals are about three times wider than the all-ball ones.
How it works: Log-loss and Brier, and why not accuracy
  • What: Log-loss is the average of −ln(the probability given to what happened). It is a proper scoring rule: the expected score is best when a model reports its true beliefs, so it can’t be gamed by exaggerating.
  • How harsh it is: Giving what happened 50% scores 0.69, 20% scores 1.61, and 1% scores 4.61. Confident mistakes cost the most.
  • Why not accuracy: The single most likely outcome is almost always a dot or a single, so every model would “predict” those and get about the same accuracy. Accuracy throws away the probabilities, and the probabilities are what the question asks about.
  • Reading the number: e−log-loss is the geometric-mean chance given to what happened. For XGBoost at the 2026 death that is e−1.5680 = 20.85%, against 20.15% for B1.
  • Brier: The squared distance between the seven probabilities and the outcome written as seven 0s and 1s: Σ(pk − yk)². It is also proper, but bounded and gentler on rare outcomes. It is used as a check, and it agrees with log-loss on every comparison here.
How it works: The paired match bootstrap
  • What: To get an interval for a difference, the 71 matches are resampled with replacement 2,000 times, and the mean difference is recomputed on each resample. The middle 95% of those 2,000 values is the interval.
  • Why whole matches: Balls in one match aren’t independent: they share a pitch, teams, weather and the match’s own run of events. Treating 3,583 balls as independent would make the intervals too narrow, so whole matches are resampled to keep that dependence.
  • Why paired: Both models are scored on the same balls, so the hard balls are hard for both. Taking the difference ball by ball cancels that shared noise.
  • What it showed: XGBoost’s own death interval [1.5460, 1.5899] overlaps B1’s [1.5805, 1.6237], yet the paired difference is −0.0338 [−0.0468, −0.0212], clearly below zero. Comparing two separate intervals would have wrongly said “can’t tell”.
How it works: Many comparisons: Bonferroni
  • What: Each 95% interval has a 5% chance of a false alarm. With 8 tests, the chance of at least one false alarm can reach about 8 × 5% = 40%. Bonferroni runs each test at 5% / 8 instead, which widens the interval from z = 1.96 to z ≈ 2.73.
  • The choice: It is used for the planned paired table (4 comparisons × 2 sets of balls) and the Shapley table. It is conservative when the tests are correlated, as these are, so clearing it is strong evidence, while missing it doesn’t prove there is no effect.
  • What it showed: XGBoost vs the logistic regression clears it at the death but not on all balls.
How it works: Pre-registration and the single test run
  • What: The claims and planned comparisons were written down and tagged in Git (freeze-v1, Oct 30) before any 2026 score was seen, and 2026 was then scored once (test-2026, Oct 31).
  • Why: With enough analysis choices, something always looks significant (the “garden of forking paths”). Fixing the questions before seeing the answers keeps the test honest.
  • What it means here: “Planned” results test a claim. The ladder, B1+, the Shapley shares, the block ablation and the error checks came later, so they describe 2026 rather than test it. That is why the site keeps the two apart.

6. Results

  • XGBoost gives the best probabilities on 2026 death balls: better than the scorecard baseline and better than the logistic regression.
  • The largest part of its gain comes from reading the scorecard more finely (53% at the death, averaged over every order). In-match context gives a clear second part (35%); player type and history can’t be told from zero at the death.
  • No single feature group is needed at the death, because the groups overlap. Removing all in-match context together does hurt.
  • At the death XGBoost predicts too few dots and too many wickets. On all balls its probabilities are close to honest.

History helps on all balls, but not consistently at the death. Adding the history of earlier matches gives 29% of XGBoost’s gain on all 2026 balls, with its interval clear of zero. The two seasons disagree, so the death result for history is inconsistent across seasons rather than a clear absence.

How it works: Ablation vs Shapley: who gets credit when features overlap
  • What: An ablation removes a group, refits, and measures how much worse the model gets. That measures each group’s unique contribution.
  • The problem: Groups overlap. Momentum and striker form both reflect recent scoring, for example. Remove one and the other covers for it, so each looks unneeded although removing both hurts. That is what happened at the death: no single group clears zero, but dropping all in-match context does (+0.0057 [+0.0012, +0.0100]). On all balls, with far more data, 8 of the 10 groups clearly help.
  • The ladder’s problem: Adding kinds of information one at a time gives the credit to whichever comes first: the finer scorecard gets 82% of the death gain when added first and 29% when added last.
  • Shapley values: Refit on all 24 = 16 combinations of the four kinds of information, and average each kind’s gain over all 4! = 24 orders of adding them. The shares are fair to overlap and add up to the total gain. At the death: scorecard 53%, in-match context 35%, history 10% and player type 2%.

What the Results Mean

  • How well: XGBoost is about 2% better than the scorecard baseline on each death ball. That is small but clearly real, and about what to expect when a single ball is mostly chance.
  • Where it comes from: the largest part comes from reading the scorecard more finely, such as the exact balls and wickets left (53% at the death, averaged over every order). In-match context (momentum, chase pressure, partnership, the bowler’s spell) gives a clear second part (35%). Player type and history can’t be told from zero at the 2026 death. Measured against the finer scorecard (B1+), all the detailed context together adds about 0.4%, too little to tell from zero on one season.
  • How honest the probabilities are: close to honest on all balls, but not at the death, where XGBoost expects too few dots and too many wickets. The death would need its own calibration.

7. Errors

The worst-predicted death balls fall into five patterns. Each is written up as a hypothesis on the Worst-Predicted Balls page, and the Match Replay shows four whole death overs ball by ball.

  1. Threes are rare for every model: 7 in 3,583 death balls, so every model gives them well under 1%.
  2. A new batter is expected to play himself in, so early sixes surprise the model, most of all from tailenders.
  3. The last balls of an innings are read as an all-out slog, so dots and singles surprise it.
  4. Some wickets have no clear cause and look like chance.
  5. Set big hitters aren’t expected to run twos.

Checked across the season. Each pattern was then tested on all 3,583 death balls of 2026, comparing how often the outcome happened with how often XGBoost predicted it:

  • Patterns 2, 3 and 5 don’t show up across the season. New batters hit sixes, the last over gave dots and singles, and set big hitters ran twos about as often as XGBoost predicted. The worst balls behind them are more likely rare outcomes than blind spots in the model.
  • Patterns 1 and 4 match the season. XGBoost predicts threes about as often as they happen (0.16% against 0.20%), and early in the death with wickets in hand its wicket chance matches what happened.
  • The one season-wide miss is the dot gap: XGBoost under-predicts dots across overs 16-19, not just in the last over (Figure 6).
How it works: What a push is (SHAP values for trees)
  • What: For each outcome, XGBoost’s raw score for a ball splits exactly into a base value plus one contribution per feature. These are SHAP values, from XGBoost’s pred_contribs. A push is a feature’s contribution to the score of the outcome that happened.
  • How to read one: A push of −0.7 lowers that outcome’s score by 0.7, which multiplies its weight by e−0.7 ≈ 0.5 before softmax re-balances the seven outcomes, so its chance is roughly halved. The pushes add up exactly to the model’s score.
  • What they are not: A push says why the model gave its number, not why the ball happened. “The No. 11 at the other end pushed singles down” is a statement about XGBoost, not about cricket.
  • What it showed: The worst balls are mostly rare outcomes that the model had reasonable grounds to doubt. The season-wide checks found one real miss, dots at the death, and no blind spot behind the single-ball patterns.

Free hits: XGBoost gives a free-hit ball about a 6% chance of a wicket, although a batter can only be run out on a free hit. There were 60 free hits in 2026, 21 of them at the death, and no wickets on any of them. The logistic regression does better on these balls.

What the Errors Say

  • The worst balls are rare outcomes, such as threes, sixes from new batters and dots on the final balls. Checked across the season, none of the patterns behind them showed up as a blind spot.
  • The one systematic miss is too few dots at the death. A calibration step fitted on death balls alone could fix it.
  • Free hits could be handled by a rule rather than learned: only a run-out can dismiss the batter, and there are too few free hits for the model to learn that.

8. Limitations

The three that matter most for the conclusions:

  • One test season. With 3,583 death balls, the death intervals are wide, so small differences, such as those between single feature groups, can’t be told apart.
  • The death calibration is off. Too few dots and too many wickets matter most for anyone using the probabilities directly, for example in a match simulator.
  • Some 2026 results are descriptive. After the single scoring run, 2026 was looked at again for the ladder, B1+, the Shapley shares, the block ablation, the error analysis, the player tables and the hypothesis checks. Nothing in the models was changed, but those results describe 2026 rather than test it.

Others:

  • 2026 outcome shares (such as the six rate) were viewed during data exploration, and the proposal quotes them. No model scores on 2026 were viewed before the freeze.
  • The logistic regression’s vector scaling was learned on 2025 predictions of the 2008-2024 model, then applied to the 2008-2025 refit. XGBoost’s number of trees was chosen the same way. Both assume the refit behaves like the model they were chosen for.
  • Player labels aren’t perfect. The hand check of 100 players found no errors, but at least one label outside it is wrong: VG Arora is labelled left-arm pace, though he bowls right-arm (228 balls in 2026). The models were frozen with this label.
  • Free-hit wickets get about a 6% chance from XGBoost, which is far too high.
  • Wides and no-balls aren’t predicted, because they are re-bowled. They are told apart by whether the ball counts as faced, not by the raw extra_type column.
  • The model barely separates death bowlers: swapping the bowler changes the expected runs by only about 0.11 per ball.
  • The close-chase replay ends in a tie that KKR won in a super over, which isn’t in the ball-by-ball data.
  • The four replay matches were picked by a fixed rule, written down after some per-match results had been seen.

9. Conclusion

XGBoost predicts a 2026 death ball about 2% better than the basic match situation (B1). The largest part comes from reading the scorecard more finely, which a basic match situation can’t do. The detailed context the question asks about matters less: in-match context gives a clear second part in every order it is added, but against a finer scorecard (B1+) all the detailed context together adds only about 0.4%, which one season can’t tell from zero. Player type and the history of earlier matches can’t be told from zero at the 2026 death, though history did help at the 2025 death and is the largest part across the whole innings.

  • Claim 1, beating the match-situation baseline at the death: met.
  • Claim 2, calibrated probabilities for each outcome: not met. At the death dots are under-predicted and wickets over-predicted; on all balls 6 of 7 outcomes are within error.
  • Claim 3, ablations show which groups the gain comes from: partially met. On all balls the planned one-group-at-a-time ablation shows it: 8 of the 10 groups clearly help, batter history the most. At the death it can’t, because the groups overlap. Analyses added after the test, which average over every order, point to the finer scorecard first (53%) and in-match context second (35%).

What would come next:

  • A calibration step for the death alone, to close the dot and wicket gaps.
  • A second test season, to narrow the death intervals and settle whether history helps at the death.
  • A Monte Carlo chase simulator built on these probabilities, once the death calibration is fixed.

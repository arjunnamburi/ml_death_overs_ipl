For every legal ball in overs 16-20, the models give a probability to each of seven outcomes: a dot, 1, 2, 3, 4, 6 or a wicket. They were trained on 2008-2024, chosen on 2025 and scored once on the 2026 season.

Source: data/results_2026.csv; XGBoost (scorecard only) is the “scorecard state” row of data/ladder_2026.csv.

Against B1, the scorecard baseline, XGBoost’s gain is 2.1%, the figure used in the Answer in Short.

In plain terms: on an average 2026 death ball, XGBoost gave what actually happened a 20.85% chance, against 20.15% for B1 (geometric means). 3,583 death balls in 71 matches.

Each section has collapsed How it works boxes that explain the methods behind it: what each one is, why it was used here instead of the alternative, and what it showed in this project.

1. Research Question

Using only information available before each delivery, how well can the probabilities of the seven outcomes of a death-over IPL delivery (0, 1, 2, 3, 4 or 6 runs, or a wicket) be predicted for an unseen season (2026), and how much of that comes from detailed context (batter, bowler, matchup history, momentum, chase pressure) beyond the basic match situation of phase, wickets fallen and innings?

The last five overs decide most T20 matches, and they are the hardest phase to predict, because the seven outcomes are spread most evenly there (Figure 2). A good probability for each death ball is the building block for decisions such as who should bowl the 19th over.

One row is one legal delivery. Each row holds what was known before the ball (the match situation, the players and their history, and the recent balls) and, as the label, what happened on it. Wides and no-balls are not rows, because they are bowled again.

The Answer in Short

  • How well: XGBoost beats the basic match situation (B1: phase, wickets band and innings) on 2026 death balls by 2.1% in log-loss, clearly more than chance.
  • Where it comes from: mostly from reading the scorecard more finely, such as the exact balls and wickets left: 53% of the death gain averaged over every order, then in-match context with 35%.
  • The detailed context: beyond XGBoost (scorecard only), which reads that finer scorecard, all the detailed context adds only 0.4% at the death, too little to tell from zero in one season.

The three claims in the proposal and their verdicts are at the start of Results.

2. Data

  • Ball numbers: balls are written the cricket way: 15.2 means the 2nd ball of the 16th over.
  • Source: the Kaggle “IPL Dataset” by Chaitanya (IPL.csv, CC0 public domain), with every IPL match from 2008 to 2026 despite its name. In the raw file one row is one delivery, wides and no-balls included: 295,732 rows and 64 columns (295,553 after removing 175 super-over rows and the 7th ball of 4 overs that had seven legal balls).
  • Key variables: the match and season, the innings, over and ball, the batter, non-striker and bowler, the runs off the bat and extras, the dismissal, and running totals for the team, batter and bowler. All 64 columns are listed in Data checks.

A random sample of 100 labelled players was checked by hand against their ESPNcricinfo profiles, and none was wrong.   • Left out: 175 super-over rows; wides and no-balls, which aren’t legal balls (they are used only to build features); and 50 rain-affected matches, whose targets were revised, scored separately in Errors.

How it works: Why the test is a later season, not a random sample
  • What: A time-based split trains on earlier seasons and tests on a later one. A random split would mix balls from every season into both sets.
  • Why here: The game drifts (Figure 1): sixes nearly doubled from 2008 to 2026. A random split would let the model see 2026-style balls in training, so the test would look better than real use, where the model always predicts a season it hasn’t seen. Splitting by season also keeps every match whole, because balls in one match share a pitch, teams and conditions.
  • The choice: Train on 2008-2024, choose between models on 2025 (validation), and score 2026 once. Choosing on a separate season keeps the test untouched: any choice made by looking at a set makes that set’s score optimistic.
  • What it showed: The drift still matters. Trained up to 2025, XGBoost still under-predicts 2026 dots at the death, and calibration learned on one season didn’t carry over to the next (see the calibration box in section 4).
  • Trees can’t follow a trend: A tree’s output is constant beyond its last split, so XGBoost treats a new season like the most similar seasons it was trained on and can’t carry a rising trend forward. The logistic regression can, but only in a straight line. That can’t explain the 2026 dot gap, though: death dots rose from 19.2% in 2025 to 21.8% in 2026, against the long trend, so a model that carried the trend forward would have missed by more. The dot gap looks like a change in 2026 itself rather than a failure to extrapolate, although this was not tested.
How it works: Entropy: how hard a phase is to guess
  • What: For outcome shares p1 … p7, entropy is H = −Σ pk ln pk. It is 0 when one outcome always happens and largest, ln 7 ≈ 1.95, when all seven are equally likely.
  • Link to log-loss: A model that knows only a phase’s true outcome shares, and gives every ball in the phase those shares, scores a log-loss equal to the entropy. So entropy is the score of “knowing the phase and nothing else”.
  • What it showed: The death is the most even phase (1.60), so it is the hardest to guess. B1 scores 1.6017 at the 2026 death, about that level. XGBoost’s 1.5680 is below it, so the ball-level features tell it something beyond the phase.
  • Entropy plus a drift penalty: A fixed set of shares q, learned on past seasons, scores H(p) + KL(p‖q) on balls whose true shares are p. The KL divergence is never negative and is zero only when q = p, so any mismatch, including drift between seasons, adds to the score. B0 gives every death ball the all-overs training shares (31% dots, against 22% at the 2026 death), so it scores 1.6434 against the 2026 death entropy of 1.6011: its KL penalty is 0.042. B1 is not one fixed set of shares but one per cell, so it can gain from its cells and pay a penalty at once; at 1.6017 the two roughly cancel.

3. Preprocessing

  • The label is one of seven outcomes on a legal ball: 0, 1, 2, 3, 4, 6 or W. It is the ball’s total runs, because that is what changes the score, so byes and leg-byes count (5 runs count as 4, and 7 as 6). In 2026, 7 legal balls with 5 runs were labelled 4, and none had 7. B1 uses the same label.
  • Wickets override runs: a run-out after a completed run is labelled W (15 such balls in 2026: 14 after one run and 1 after two). Retired hurt is not a wicket.
  • The death is every legal ball after the first 90 legal balls of an innings, which is from over 16.
  • Features are built only from earlier balls, so nothing about the ball being predicted leaks in.
  • History features (batter, bowler, matchup and venue) use a three-season window: the two previous seasons plus the current season before the match. Each rate is pulled toward a group average, so a player with few balls stays close to it: (balls × own rate + 100 × group rate) / (balls + 100). Batters are pulled toward their batting-position group (top 1-3, middle 4-5, finisher 6-8, tail 9-11), bowlers toward their bowling type in that phase, and venues toward the league. The strength of 100 balls was fixed, the same as in B1, rather than tuned, so that 2025 was kept for comparing the models.
  • For the logistic regression, the 87 features become 118 inputs: one column per category for the 5 categorical features, flags for missing values, clipping and standardising. The flags, clipping limits and scaling are learned on the training seasons only, and re-learned on 2008-2025 for the refit.
  • The impact-player rule (from 2023) is a feature, impact_player_era, in the Venue and season group. In 2026 it is 1 on every ball, so it only tells the model which era a training ball came from. It ranks 38th of 87 by average push at the 2026 death.
  • No recent-form features (a player’s last few matches) were built: such short windows hold too few balls and are too noisy, so the three-season history stands in for form.
How it works: Leakage: only information from before the ball
  • What: Leakage is a feature that holds information not known when the prediction is made. The classic case is a running total that already includes the ball being predicted.
  • Why it matters: A leaked feature makes a model look far better on the test than it could ever be in use. In ball-by-ball data it hides easily: the raw file’s running totals for the team, batter and bowler are taken after the ball.
  • The choice: Every in-match feature is built from earlier balls only, so running totals are shifted back one ball. History windows use the two previous seasons plus the current season before this match, never the match itself.
  • The red flags: Leakage makes a model look too good: a score better than plausible, or one feature carrying an implausible share of the predictions. Neither appeared. XGBoost’s 1.568 at the death is only a little below the 1.60 entropy of the phase, and the feature that moves XGBoost most accounts for only 9% of the total size of the pushes at the 2026 death. (A richer model doing worse than a simpler one would point to a bug or overfitting, not to leakage.)
  • A direct check: After a double-counted delivery was found (section 2), the running totals were rebuilt from the balls themselves rather than taken from the raw file. Then, for all 74 matches of 2026 (17,523 deliveries, rain-affected matches included), every running total in the feature table (the team’s, batter’s, bowler’s and non-striker’s runs and balls, and the team’s and bowler’s wickets) was recomputed from earlier balls only, and it matched on every row; the first ball of all 147 innings starts from zero. The windowed features (last 6 and 12 balls, and history) weren’t rechecked this way.
How it works: Shrinkage: trusting a rate only as much as its sample allows
  • What: A rate from a few balls is noisy: 2 sixes in 10 balls is a 20% six rate that means little. Shrinkage blends a player’s own rate with a group rate: (balls × own + k × group) / (balls + k), with k = 100.
  • How to read k: k acts like 100 imaginary balls at the group rate. The weight on the player’s own rate is balls / (balls + 100): 17% with 20 balls, 50% with 100 and 80% with 400.
  • Why: It is the bias-variance trade-off. The raw rate is unbiased but has a huge variance for new players. The group rate has a small variance but ignores the player. The blend can beat both when k is well chosen; k wasn’t tuned here.
  • Where the formula comes from: For a yes/no rate, such as the six rate, it is the posterior mean under a Beta prior whose mean is the group rate and whose strength is k balls. Full empirical Bayes would estimate both the prior’s mean and its strength from the data. Here only the mean comes from the data, and k = 100 was set by hand, so it is only half empirical Bayes.
  • The choice: Batters shrink toward their batting-position group, bowlers toward their bowling type in that phase, and venues toward the league. k was fixed at 100, as in B1, rather than tuned, so that 2025 stayed free for comparing the models.
How it works: Seven outcomes as unordered classes
  • What: Each ball gets one of seven labels (0, 1, 2, 3, 4, 6, W), and the models treat them as seven separate classes with no order between them. One softmax gives the seven chances, which always add up to 1, and log-loss scores them together.
  • The alternative: Runs as an ordered count (0 < 1 < 2 < 3 < 4 < 6), with an ordinal or count model, plus a separate model for the wicket.
  • Why not here: Runs off a ball aren’t a smooth count. Fours and sixes come from boundaries, not from running, so a 4 is far more common than a 3 (13.3% against 0.2% of 2026 death balls) and a 5 almost never happens. A count model ties neighbouring values together and would spread chance onto threes and fives. A wicket ends the ball’s runs (a run-out after a completed run is still W), so it competes with the run outcomes rather than happening alongside them, and two separate models would not add up to 1 without extra work.
  • The cost: The models don’t know that a 4 is “closer” to a 6 than to a dot, so each class’s pattern is learned from its own balls. With about 260,000 training balls that is enough for every class except threes.

How it works: Why baselines first
  • What: A baseline is the simplest model a claim has to beat. B0 gives every ball the outcome shares of the training seasons. B1 gives each cell (phase × wickets band × innings) its own shares, shrunk toward B0 with the same k = 100.
  • Why: A log-loss of 1.568 means nothing on its own. It only means something next to what a simple model scores. B1 is the “basic match situation” of the research question, so XGBoost minus B1 is the question’s answer.
  • What it showed: B1 already gains 0.0417 over B0 at the death, more than XGBoost then adds on top (0.0338). Much of what can be predicted about a death ball is the match situation. XGBoost (scorecard only) measures what a finer reading of that situation is worth.
How it works: Multinomial logistic regression
  • What: For each outcome k the model computes a score zk = wk · x + bk, a weighted sum of the inputs. Softmax turns the seven scores into probabilities: pk = ezk / Σj ezj. The weights are fitted by minimising log-loss on the training balls, plus an L2 penalty that keeps them small (C = 1; a smaller C means a stronger penalty).
  • What it can’t do: Each feature moves the score by the same amount everywhere. “Wickets in hand matter more at the death” is an interaction, and the model can’t learn it unless such a feature is built by hand.
  • Why the preprocessing:
    • Categories become one-hot columns, because a weight needs a number.
    • Missing values get a flag column plus a fill value, because the model can’t take a blank.
    • Extreme values are clipped at the training 0.5th and 99.5th percentiles, so a few outliers don’t swing a weight.
    • Inputs are standardised, so the L2 penalty treats every feature on the same scale and the solver (L-BFGS) converges.
    • All of these are learned on the training seasons only; learning them on the test season would be a leak.
  • Its role: The strong simple model. Its gap to XGBoost is what curves and interactions add: −0.0084 [−0.0143, −0.0029] at the 2026 death.
How it works: Gradient-boosted trees (XGBoost)
  • What: Boosting builds many small trees in sequence, each correcting what the trees before it got wrong. With seven outcomes, each round adds one tree per outcome. Each tree is fitted to the gradient of log-loss (predicted probability minus 1 if that outcome happened, else minus 0), using the curvature too. Its output, times the learning rate, is added to that outcome’s score, and softmax turns the scores into probabilities, as in the logistic regression.
  • What each setting controls:
    • Depth 4: a tree can combine up to four features, so it learns interactions such as balls left × wickets in hand × required rate.
    • Minimum child weight 100: a leaf needs a total curvature of 100, which is a few hundred balls for common outcomes and far more for rare ones like threes, so leaves stay stable.
    • Learning rate 0.05: each tree takes a small step, which needs more trees but overfits less.
    • Row and column sampling 0.8: each tree sees a random 80% of balls and features, which adds variety and reduces overfitting.
    • Early stopping and the 301 rounds: trained on 2008-2024, rounds were added until the 2025 loss hadn’t improved for 50 rounds. 301 is the best round (best iteration + 1), not the round where training stopped, 50 rounds later. The final model was refit on 2008-2025 (about 6% more balls) with the same 301 rounds, with no adjustment for the extra data. Each round adds one tree per outcome, so it has 301 × 7 = 2,107 trees.
    • Categories: the 5 categorical features (batting hand, bowling type, matchup type, match phase and the previous ball’s outcome) use XGBoost’s native support (enable_categorical=True on pandas category columns). A split sends a set of categories one way and the rest the other, instead of cutting arbitrary number codes, which would invent an order. Every season uses the same list of categories, so a category means the same thing in training and in the test.
  • The choice: A first fit at depth 6 with minimum child weight 1 overfit: its training loss was 0.115 below its 2024 loss, and it stopped after 135 rounds. The grid on 2024 then moved to shallower trees and bigger leaves (depth 3, 4, 6 × minimum child weight 20, 100). All six settings scored within 0.0014 of each other, bigger leaves were better at every depth, and depth 4 with 100 won. Missing values need no flags: each split learns which way to send them.
  • What it showed: It beats the logistic regression most at the death, where interactions matter most: −0.0084 there against −0.0026 on all balls.
How it works: Why rare outcomes weren’t given more weight
  • The imbalance: At the 2026 death, singles are 39% of balls and threes 0.2%. A common fix for imbalance is class weights, which make each rare ball count more in training.
  • Why not here: The goal is honest probabilities, scored by log-loss. Log-loss is a proper scoring rule: its expected value is lowest when the predicted chances equal the true frequencies. Weighting threes by 50 changes what is being fitted: the best answer becomes a three chance about 50 times too high (before renormalising). The model would then over-predict threes on every ball, and pay for it on the other 99.8%. Reweighting suits a goal like catching rare events (recall), not giving their chance.
  • What it means: Rare outcomes get small but honest chances. At the 2026 death XGBoost predicted threes on 0.16% of balls against 0.20% observed. When a three does happen it costs a large log-loss (the first pattern in Errors), and that is the price of honest probabilities, not a bug.

Calibration

The logistic regression uses vector scaling learned on 2025. XGBoost stays raw: every fix tried on 2025, including the temperature scaling the proposal planned, made it worse (+0.0010 to +0.0022 per ball). For the calibration options table, see Extras.

How it works: Calibration: temperature vs vector scaling
  • What: A model is calibrated when its 10% predictions happen about 10% of the time. The reliability diagram (Figure 5) checks this by grouping balls by predicted chance. Calibration is separate from discrimination, which is telling risky balls from safe ones: a model that gives every ball the season shares is calibrated but useless. Log-loss rewards both.
  • The two fixes, both fitted on held-out predictions, never on the training balls:
    • Temperature scaling: pk = softmax(zk / T). One number: T > 1 softens over-confident predictions and T < 1 sharpens them, but it can’t fix one outcome being too low overall.
    • Vector scaling: pk = softmax(ak zk + bk). Fourteen numbers, a scale and a shift per outcome, so it can also fix “too few dots, too many wickets”.
  • The choice: Each fix was learned on one season and judged on the next (2025). Vector scaling helped the logistic regression, whose errors are systematic because it is linear. It hurt XGBoost (+0.0019 on all balls, +0.0022 at the death), even though it had helped on the season it was learned on (2024): the correction fitted that season’s shift, which didn’t carry over. So XGBoost stays raw.
  • What it showed: XGBoost is close to calibrated on all balls, but at the 2026 death it under-predicts dots (2.23 points) and over-predicts wickets (1.01 points). A fix learned on 2025 couldn’t have caught that. At the 2025 death, the model trained on 2008-2024 had no dot gap (+0.56 points [−0.65, +1.79]); it under-predicted singles (−3.48 [−5.35, −1.61]) and over-predicted sixes (+1.48 [+0.50, +2.36]). A death-only fix learned there would have moved singles and sixes, not dots.

Tried and Not Adopted

Giving recent seasons more weight (8 options) was no better on 2025, and neither death-only model replaced the all-overs one. For the table, and why recency weights didn’t help, see Extras.

How it works: Why one model for all overs
  • What: A model trained on death balls only can learn death-specific patterns, but from about 4.4 times fewer balls. A model trained on all overs has more data and can still tell the death apart through features such as balls left.
  • Bias and variance: This is the bias-variance trade-off. The death-only model has lower bias at the death, because it fits death balls alone, but higher variance, because fewer balls make its estimates move more with the luck of the sample. The all-overs model has lower variance, but it gains bias if it can’t fully separate the death from other overs, since patterns from the powerplay and middle overs then leak into its death predictions. Which side wins depends on the model. XGBoost’s trees can split on the phase, so its all-overs model loses little to bias. The logistic regression gives each feature the same weight in every over, so its all-overs version carries more bias. That fits 2025: the death-only logistic regression was better at the death (−0.0072 [−0.0121, −0.0022]), but the death-only XGBoost wasn’t.
  • The rule: A death-only model is used only if its 2025 death-ball interval against the all-overs model is entirely below zero.
  • What it showed: For XGBoost, death-only minus all-overs was +0.0004 [−0.0031, +0.0040], so the all-overs model was kept: depth-4 trees already split on the phase where it matters.

5. Evaluation

  • Log-loss (the headline) is the average of −log(the probability given to what actually happened). Lower is better. A model that gives the real outcome a 20% chance scores 1.61 on that ball.
  • Brier score (a check) is the squared distance between the seven probabilities and what happened. It agrees with log-loss on every comparison here.
  • Two models are always compared on the same balls, and the difference is what’s reported.
How to read the intervals A difference is written as −0.0338 [−0.0468, −0.0212]. The first number is the difference on 2026. The two numbers in brackets are a 95% interval: the range the difference would likely fall in if 2026 had been played with a different set of matches. It comes from resampling the 71 matches 2,000 times, whole matches at a time, because balls in one match aren’t independent.
  • If the whole interval is below zero, the first model is better, and chance is an unlikely explanation.
  • If the interval crosses zero, we can’t tell the two apart on this data.
  • When many comparisons are made at once, a stricter version of the interval is used (Bonferroni). The paired table has 8 planned tests (4 comparisons on 2 sets of balls), so its stricter interval is about 1.4 times as wide (z ≈ 2.73 instead of 1.96). The tables say when an interval also clears that.
  • What the tests can show: that one model gave better probabilities than another on 2026 death balls, and by how much.
  • What they can’t show: why it was better, whether it will hold in other seasons, or anything about cause and effect on the field. With 3,583 death balls, the death intervals are about three times wider than the all-ball ones.
How it works: Log-loss and Brier, and why not accuracy
  • What: Log-loss is the average of −ln(the probability given to what happened). It is a proper scoring rule: the expected score is best when a model reports its true beliefs, so it can’t be gamed by exaggerating.
  • How harsh it is: Giving what happened 50% scores 0.69, 20% scores 1.61, and 1% scores 4.61. Confident mistakes cost the most.
  • Why not accuracy: The single most likely outcome is almost always a dot or a single, so every model would “predict” those and get about the same accuracy. Accuracy throws away the probabilities, and the probabilities are what the question asks about.
  • Reading the number: e−log-loss is the geometric-mean chance given to what happened. For XGBoost at the 2026 death that is e−1.5680 = 20.85%, against 20.15% for B1.
  • Brier: The squared distance between the seven probabilities and the outcome written as seven 0s and 1s: Σ(pk − yk)². It is also proper, but bounded and gentler on rare outcomes. It is used as a check, and it agrees with log-loss on every comparison here.
How it works: The paired match bootstrap
  • What: To get an interval for a difference, the 71 matches are resampled with replacement 2,000 times, and the mean difference is recomputed on each resample. The middle 95% of those 2,000 values is the interval.
  • Why whole matches: Balls in one match aren’t independent: they share a pitch, teams, weather and the match’s own run of events. Treating 3,583 balls as independent would make the intervals too narrow, so whole matches are resampled to keep that dependence.
  • Why paired: Both models are scored on the same balls, so the hard balls are hard for both. Taking the difference ball by ball cancels that shared noise.
  • What it showed: XGBoost’s own death interval [1.5460, 1.5899] overlaps B1’s [1.5805, 1.6237], yet the paired difference is −0.0338 [−0.0468, −0.0212], clearly below zero. Comparing two separate intervals would have wrongly said “can’t tell”.
How it works: Many comparisons: Bonferroni
  • What: Each 95% interval has a 5% chance of a false alarm. With 8 tests, the chance of at least one false alarm is at most 8 × 5% = 40% (the union bound), and about 1 − 0.958 ≈ 34% if the tests were independent. Bonferroni runs each test at 5% / 8 instead, which widens the interval from z = 1.96 to z ≈ 2.73.
  • The choice: It is used for the planned paired table (4 comparisons × 2 sets of balls, z ≈ 2.73), the planned slices (22 groups × 2 comparisons = 44 tests, z ≈ 3.26), the two planned failure modes (2 × 2 = 4 tests, z ≈ 2.50) and the Shapley table (8 tests). It is conservative when the tests are correlated, as these are, so clearing it is strong evidence, while missing it doesn’t prove there is no effect.
  • What it showed: XGBoost vs the logistic regression clears it at the death in the 8-test paired family, but not on all balls, and none of the slice claims against the logistic regression clears the 44-test bar.
How it works: Pre-registration and the single test run
  • What: The claims and planned comparisons were written down and tagged in Git (freeze-v1, Oct 30) before any 2026 score was seen, and 2026 was then scored once (test-2026, Oct 31).
  • Why: With enough analysis choices, something always looks significant (the “garden of forking paths”). Fixing the questions before seeing the answers keeps the test honest.
  • What it means here: “Planned” results test a claim. The ladder, XGBoost (scorecard only), the Shapley shares, the block ablation and the error checks came later, so they describe 2026 rather than test it. That is why the site keeps the two apart.

How it works: Ablation vs Shapley: who gets credit when features overlap
  • What: An ablation removes a group, refits, and measures how much worse the model gets. That measures each group’s unique contribution.
  • The problem: Groups overlap. Momentum and striker form both reflect recent scoring, for example. Remove one and the other covers for it, so each looks unneeded although removing both hurts. That is what happened at the death: no single group clears zero, but dropping all in-match context does (+0.0057 [+0.0012, +0.0100]). On all balls, with far more data, 8 of the 10 groups clearly help.
  • The ladder’s problem: Adding kinds of information one at a time gives the credit to whichever comes first: the finer scorecard gets 82% of the death gain when added first and 29% when added last.
  • Shapley values: Refit on all 24 = 16 combinations of the four kinds of information, and average each kind’s gain over all 4! = 24 orders of adding them. The shares are fair to overlap and add up to the total gain. At the death: scorecard 53%, in-match context 35%, history 10% and player type 2%.

The Top Feature: Balls Left per Wicket in Hand

This is the number of balls left in the innings divided by the wickets the batting side has left. It measures how much a wicket costs. With 12 balls left, a side with 6 wickets in hand has 2 balls per wicket and can swing at everything, because losing a wicket costs little. With 1 wicket in hand it has 12 balls per wicket and has to protect it. So one number carries the two things a batter weighs before every ball: how many balls are left and how many wickets are left.

Of all 87 features, it moves XGBoost’s predictions the most. Its average push size (what a push is) on 2026 balls is 0.083, just ahead of legal balls bowled so far (0.082) and well ahead of the next feature, the striker’s six rate over the last 3 seasons (0.048). At the death its lead grows (0.109 against 0.071). The 2026 balls show why it matters:

7. Errors

The worst-predicted death balls fall into five patterns. Each is written up as a hypothesis on the Worst-Predicted Balls page, and the Match Replay shows four whole death overs ball by ball.

  1. Threes are rare for every model: 7 in 3,583 death balls, so every model gives them well under 1%.
  2. A new batter is expected to play himself in, so early sixes surprise the model, most of all from tailenders.
  3. The last balls of an innings are read as an all-out slog, so dots and singles surprise it.
  4. Some wickets have no clear cause and look like chance.
  5. Set big hitters aren’t expected to run twos.

Checked across the season. Each pattern was then tested on all 3,583 death balls of 2026, comparing how often the outcome happened with how often XGBoost predicted it:

  • Patterns 2, 3 and 5 don’t show up across the season. New batters hit sixes, the last over gave dots and singles, and set big hitters ran twos about as often as XGBoost predicted. The worst balls behind them are more likely rare outcomes than blind spots in the model.
  • Patterns 1 and 4 match the season. XGBoost predicts threes about as often as they happen (0.16% against 0.20%), and early in the death with wickets in hand its wicket chance matches what happened.
  • The one season-wide miss is the dot gap: XGBoost under-predicts dots across overs 16-19, not just in the last over (Figure 5).
How it works: What a push is (SHAP values for trees)
  • What: For each outcome, XGBoost’s raw score for a ball splits exactly into a base value plus one contribution per feature. These are SHAP values, computed exactly for trees by TreeSHAP (XGBoost’s pred_contribs). A push is a feature’s contribution to the score of the outcome that happened.
  • How to read one: A push of −0.7 lowers that outcome’s score by 0.7. It is a change in a score, not in a chance. Softmax turns all seven scores into chances together, pk = ezk / Σj ezj, so the chance of what happened also depends on the pushes on the other six scores. Only for an unlikely outcome whose rivals’ scores barely move does −0.7 come close to halving its chance (e−0.7 ≈ 0.5).
  • The exact chance: So each ball on the Worst-Predicted Balls page gives the chance itself: XGBoost’s chance of what happened at its starting point (the softmax of the seven base values: 30.5% for a dot, 40.0% for a single, 6.6% for a two, 0.3% for a three, 12.2% for a four, 5.3% for a six and 5.1% for a wicket), and the chance after all the pushes.
  • What they are not: A push says why the model gave its number, not why the ball happened. “The No. 11 at the other end pushed singles down” is a statement about XGBoost, not about cricket.
  • What it showed: The worst balls are mostly rare outcomes that the model had reasonable grounds to doubt. The season-wide checks found one real miss, dots at the death, and no blind spot behind the single-ball patterns.

Free hits: XGBoost gives a free-hit ball about a 6% chance of a wicket, although a batter can only be run out on a free hit. There were 60 free hits in 2026, 21 of them at the death, and no wickets on any of them. The logistic regression scores better on these balls, by 0.046 per ball, but with 60 balls the interval just reaches zero (XGBoost − logistic regression +0.0457 [−0.0014, +0.0914]).

How it works: Why XGBoost can’t learn free hits
  • The rule it would need: Since 2016 there has been no bowler wicket on 666 free hits, so the wicket chance on a free hit should be near zero.
  • Why the trees can’t isolate them: Minimum child weight is a minimum total curvature in a leaf, not a count of balls. For XGBoost’s softmax log-loss, each ball adds 2p(1 − p) to the tree for an outcome with chance p. For the wicket tree on a free hit, p ≈ 0.06, so each free hit adds about 2 × 0.06 × 0.94 ≈ 0.11. The training seasons have about 1,100 free hits, which together add about 125, barely above the leaf minimum of 100. A leaf that held only free hits would need almost all of them, after up to three other splits, so in practice no wicket-tree leaf can separate them.
  • Checked in the model: Of the 2,107 trees, 91 split on the free-hit flag (41 for fours, 41 for sixes, 7 for dots and 2 for singles), and none of the 301 wicket trees does.
  • The fix: A rule rather than learning: on a free hit, set the wicket chance to the run-out rate and rescale the other six. The logistic regression does better here because its weight on the flag applies to every ball and needs no leaf of its own.

Rain-affected matches, left out of the test, tell the same story: on all 50, XGBoost beats B1 by −0.0383 [−0.0603, −0.0164] per death ball. Most were scored by a model that had seen other matches of their season, so that gain is optimistic. For the rain table, see Extras.

8. Limitations

The four that matter most for the conclusions:

  • One test season. With 3,583 death balls, the death intervals are wide, so small differences, such as those between single feature groups, can’t be told apart.
  • The death calibration is off. Too few dots and too many wickets matter most for anyone using the probabilities directly, for example in a match simulator.
  • Some 2026 results are descriptive. After the single scoring run, 2026 was looked at again for the ladder, XGBoost (scorecard only), the Shapley shares, the block ablation, the error analysis, the player tables and the hypothesis checks. Nothing in the models was changed, but those results describe 2026 rather than test it.
  • The scorecard group holds some player information. 6 of its 17 features are about the players at the crease rather than the match: the striker’s batting position, the striker’s runs and balls so far, and the bowler’s runs, wickets and balls so far. They were grouped with the scorecard because a TV scorecard shows them. So XGBoost (scorecard only) and the scorecard’s shares (53% of the death gain, and the 0.4% that the rest adds beyond it) partly credit player information to the match situation. XGBoost (scorecard only) wasn’t refit on the 11 match-state features alone, so how much is not measured.

Others:

  • 2026 outcome shares (such as the six rate) were viewed during data exploration, and the proposal quotes them. No model scores on 2026 were viewed before the freeze.
  • The logistic regression’s vector scaling was learned on 2025 predictions of the 2008-2024 model, then applied to the 2008-2025 refit. XGBoost’s number of trees was chosen the same way. Both assume the refit behaves like the model they were chosen for.
  • Player labels aren’t perfect. The hand check of 100 players found no errors, but at least one label outside it is wrong: VG Arora is labelled left-arm pace, though he bowls right-arm (228 balls in 2026). The models were frozen with this label.
  • Free-hit wickets get about a 6% chance from XGBoost, which is far too high.
  • Wides and no-balls aren’t predicted, because they are bowled again (Data checks).
  • The model barely separates death bowlers. With 54 regular death bowlers swapped onto the same 1,494 chase balls, the middle 80% of their predicted economies lie within 0.52 runs per over. Once the overs each bowler had left at over 17 are counted, the best and worst plans for overs 17-20 differ by a median of only 0.11 runs (38 chases). The check is in bowler_swap_check.ipynb.
  • The close-chase replay ends in a tie that KKR won in a super over, which isn’t in the ball-by-ball data.
  • The four replay matches were picked by a fixed rule written on Nov 4. By then some whole-match results on all balls had been seen (the most and least predictable matches, and one match suggested during the error analysis), but no per-match death result had been looked at.

9. Conclusion

XGBoost predicts a 2026 death ball about 2% better than the basic match situation (B1). The largest part comes from reading the scorecard more finely, which B1 can’t do. The detailed context the question asks about matters less: in-match context helps in every order it is added, but beyond XGBoost (scorecard only) all the detailed context together adds only about 0.4%, which one season can’t tell from zero. Player type and history can’t be told from zero at the 2026 death, though history helped at the 2025 death and across the whole innings.

What would come next:

  • A calibration step for the death alone, to close the dot and wicket gaps.
  • A second test season, to narrow the death intervals and settle whether history helps at the death.
  • A Monte Carlo chase simulator built on these probabilities, once the death calibration is fixed.

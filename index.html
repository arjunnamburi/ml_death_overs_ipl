1. Research Question

How well can a model predict the outcome of each ball in the death overs of an IPL match (overs 16-20) in a season it has never seen, and how much of that comes from match context beyond the scorecard?

The last five overs decide most T20 matches, and they are the hardest to predict: the seven outcomes are spread more evenly there than in any other phase (Figure 2). A probability for each ball is the building block for questions such as who should bowl the 19th over. “Well” here means log-loss, which rewards giving the real outcome a high chance, measured against a scorecard baseline (B1) that knows only the phase, the wickets band and the innings.

The answer in short: XGBoost beats the scorecard baseline on 2026 death balls by 2.1%, within the 1-3% expected before the test. Most of that gain comes from reading the scorecard more finely. In-match context adds a smaller but clear part, and player history adds nothing measurable at the death.

3. Preprocessing

  • The label is one of seven outcomes on a legal ball: 0, 1, 2, 3, 4, 6 or W. It counts every run on the ball, including byes and leg-byes (5 runs count as 4, and 7 as 6).
  • The death is every legal ball after the first 90 legal balls of an innings, which is from over 16.
  • Features are built only from earlier balls, so nothing about the ball being predicted leaks in.
  • History features (batter, bowler, matchup and venue) use a three-season window: the two previous seasons plus the current season before the match. Each rate is pulled toward the average for the player’s group, so a player with few balls stays close to that average. The strength of this pull was fixed at 100 balls, the same as in B1, rather than tuned, so that 2025 was kept for comparing the models.
  • For the logistic regression, the 87 features become 118 inputs: one column per category for the 5 categorical features, flags for missing values, clipping and standardising.
  • No recent-form features (a player’s last few matches) were built: such short windows hold too few balls and are too noisy, so the three-season history stands in for form.

Why These Two Models

  • Logistic regression adds up the effect of each feature. It is the strong simple model, so its gap to XGBoost shows what curves and interactions add.
  • XGBoost builds boosted trees, which find interactions on their own (for example, wickets in hand matter more at the death). It optimises log-loss directly and handles missing values and categories without extra steps.
  • Depth 4 with large leaves won a small grid on 2024. All six settings scored within 0.0014 of each other, so the result doesn’t hang on lucky tuning. Deeper trees with small leaves overfit.
  • One model for all overs was kept, because a model trained on death balls only was no better on 2025.

5. Evaluation

  • Log-loss (the headline) is the average of −log(the probability given to what actually happened). Lower is better. A model that gives the real outcome a 20% chance scores 1.61 on that ball.
  • Brier score (a check) is the squared distance between the seven probabilities and what happened. It agrees with log-loss on every comparison here.
  • Two models are always compared on the same balls, and the difference is what’s reported.
How to read the intervals A difference is written as −0.0338 [−0.0468, −0.0212]. The first number is the difference on 2026. The two numbers in brackets are a 95% interval: the range the difference would likely fall in if 2026 had been played with a different set of matches. It comes from resampling the 71 matches 2,000 times, whole matches at a time, because balls in one match aren’t independent.
  • If the whole interval is below zero, the first model is better, and chance is an unlikely explanation.
  • If the interval crosses zero, we can’t tell the two apart on this data.
  • When many comparisons are made at once, a stricter version of the interval is used (Bonferroni). The tables say when an interval also clears that.
  • What the tests can show: that one model gave better probabilities than another on 2026 death balls, and by how much.
  • What they can’t show: why it was better, whether it will hold in other seasons, or anything about cause and effect on the field. With 3,583 death balls, the death intervals are about three times wider than the all-ball ones.

6. Results

  • XGBoost gives the best probabilities on 2026 death balls: better than the scorecard baseline and better than the logistic regression.
  • Most of its gain comes from reading the scorecard more finely. In-match context adds a clear further gain; player type and history add nothing measurable at the death.
  • No single feature group is needed at the death, because the groups overlap. Removing all in-match context does hurt.
  • The probabilities are mostly honest, but at the death every model expects too few dots.

Are the Probabilities Honest?

What the Results Mean

  • How well: XGBoost is about 2% better than the scorecard baseline on each death ball. That is small but clearly real, and about what to expect when a single ball is mostly chance.
  • Where it comes from: mostly from reading the scorecard more finely, such as the exact balls and wickets left. In-match context (momentum, chase pressure, partnership, the bowler’s spell) adds a clear further part. Player type and history add nothing measurable at the death.
  • How honest the probabilities are: mostly honest, but the death needs its own calibration. XGBoost expects too few dots there, and too many wickets on its most wicket-prone balls.

7. Errors

The worst-predicted death balls fall into four patterns. Each is written up as a hypothesis on the Worst-Predicted Balls page, and the Match Replay shows four whole death overs ball by ball.

  1. Big hitters at boundary-friendly grounds aren’t expected to run twos or threes.
  2. A new batter is expected to play himself in, so early sixes surprise the model, most of all from tailenders.
  3. The last balls of an innings are read as an all-out slog, so dots and singles surprise it.
  4. Some wickets have no clear cause and look like chance.

Checked across the season. Each pattern was then tested on all 3,583 death balls of 2026, comparing how often the outcome happened with how often XGBoost predicted it:

  • Patterns 1-3 don’t show up across the season. Big hitters ran twos and threes, new batters hit sixes, and the last over gave dots and singles about as often as XGBoost predicted. The worst balls behind them are more likely rare outcomes than blind spots in the model.
  • Pattern 4 matches the season. Early in the death with wickets in hand, XGBoost’s wicket chance matches what happened.
  • The one season-wide miss is the dot gap: XGBoost under-predicts dots across overs 16-19, not just in the last over (Figure 6).

Free hits: XGBoost gives a free-hit ball about a 6% chance of a wicket, although a batter can only be run out on a free hit. The logistic regression does better on these balls.

What the Errors Say

  • The worst balls are rare outcomes, such as threes, sixes from new batters and dots on the final balls. Checked across the season, none of the patterns behind them showed up as a blind spot.
  • The one systematic miss is too few dots at the death. A calibration step fitted on death balls alone could fix it.
  • Free hits could be handled by a rule rather than learned: only a run-out can dismiss the batter, and there are too few free hits for the model to learn that.

8. Limitations

The three that matter most for the conclusions:

  • One test season. With 3,583 death balls, the death intervals are wide, so small differences, such as those between single feature groups, can’t be told apart.
  • The death calibration is off. Too few dots matters most for anyone using the probabilities directly, for example in a match simulator.
  • Some 2026 results are descriptive. After the single scoring run, 2026 was looked at again for the error analysis, the player tables and the hypothesis checks. Nothing in the models was changed, but those results describe 2026 rather than test it.

Others:

  • Free-hit wickets get about a 6% chance from XGBoost, which is far too high.
  • The data has no column for the type of extra, so wides and no-balls are told apart by the free hit that follows a no-ball.
  • The model barely separates death bowlers: swapping the bowler changes the expected runs by only about 0.11 per ball.
  • The close-chase replay ends in a tie that KKR won in a super over, which isn’t in the ball-by-ball data.
  • The four replay matches were picked by a fixed rule, written down after some per-match results had been seen.

9. Conclusion

XGBoost predicts a 2026 death ball about 2% better than a scorecard baseline, and most of that comes from reading the match situation more finely. The detailed context the question asked about adds less. In-match context gives a clear further gain, while player type and the history of earlier matches add nothing measurable at the death, even though history helps across the whole innings.

  • Claim 1, beating the scorecard baseline at the death: met.
  • Claim 2, calibrated probabilities: met on all balls, but not at the death, where dots are under-predicted.
  • Claim 3, ablations show where the gain comes from: no single feature group is needed at the death, because the groups overlap, but in-match context as a whole is.

What would come next:

  • A calibration step for the death alone, to close the dot gap.
  • A second test season, to narrow the death intervals.
  • A Monte Carlo chase simulator built on these probabilities, once the death calibration is fixed.

10. Reproduction Steps

  1. Get the code from github.com/arjunnamburi/ml_death_overs_ipl. The frozen state is tagged test-2026.
  2. Run the notebooks in notebooks/ in this order: features, models, test_2026, post_freeze_checks, analysis_nb, error_analysis, site_export, site_build, site_figures and h1_checks.
  3. The tables are in data/, and the site’s own data and figures are in site/data/ and site/img/.
  • Files kept on Google Drive (too large for the repo): the feature table ipl_features.parquet, the fitted 2026 models xgb_X-hist_2026.json and models_2026.joblib, and the per-ball predictions bundle_2026.parquet and push_death_2026.parquet. The notebooks rebuild all of them.
  • Raw data: IPL.csv from the Kaggle “IPL Dataset” by Chaitanya (CC0). Run features on it first.
  • Packages: XGBoost 3.4.1. The notebooks were run on Google Colab.

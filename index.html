1. The question

Research question. How well can a model predict the outcome of each ball in the death overs of an IPL match (overs 16-20) on an unseen season, and how much of that comes from detailed match context beyond the scorecard?

One paragraph: why ball-level prediction at the death is interesting, what “well” means here (log-loss against the scorecard baseline), and the answer in two sentences.

3. Preprocessing

  • The label is one of seven outcomes on a legal ball: 0, 1, 2, 3, 4, 6 or W. It counts every run on the ball, including byes and leg-byes (5 runs count as 4, 7 as 6).
  • The death is every legal ball after 90 legal balls of the innings (from over 16).
  • Features are computed from the balls before the one being predicted, so nothing from the ball itself leaks in.
  • History features (batter, bowler, matchup, venue) use a three-season window: the two previous seasons plus the current season before the match. Each rate is shrunk toward its group's average with strength k = 100 balls, so a player with few balls is close to the average for his batting position.
  • For logistic regression the 87 features become 118 inputs: one-hot columns for the 5 categorical features, missing-value flags, clipping, and standardising (learned on the training seasons only).
The 12 feature groups in plain words (scorecard, player type, momentum, chase pressure, striker, partnership, bowler spell, delivery, and the four history groups), with one example feature each.

Why the logistic regression uses vector scaling

Vector scaling rescales each outcome's score with its own slope and offset, learned on held-out data. On 2025, where the choice was made, it beat the plain logistic regression by −0.0023 [−0.0038, −0.0008] per ball on all balls and −0.0050 [−0.0074, −0.0030] at the death. It did again on 2026: −0.0031 [−0.0048, −0.0012] and −0.0095 [−0.0146, −0.0043].

Why XGBoost has no calibration step

Vector scaling learned on 2024 helped XGBoost there (−0.0026), but on 2025 it made it worse: +0.0019 [+0.0008, +0.0030] on all balls and +0.0022 [+0.0004, +0.0039] at the death. The season had shifted. Temperature scaling also hurt XGBoost (+0.0010; death +0.0016) and did nothing for the logistic regression (+0.0001). So XGBoost is used raw.

Why these two learners, and why depth 4 and these settings (tuning on 2024, early stopping).

5. Evaluation

  • Log-loss (the headline): the average of −log(probability given to what actually happened). Lower is better. A model that gives the true outcome 20% scores 1.61 on that ball.
  • Brier score (a check): the squared distance between the seven probabilities and what happened. All ten paired verdicts agree with log-loss.
  • Paired match bootstrap: two models are scored on the same balls, and the difference is resampled by match (2,000 times), because balls in one match aren't independent. The 95% interval is shown in brackets.
  • Bonferroni: where many comparisons are made at once, an interval must also clear a stricter bar. The tables say when.
What each test can and can't establish: a paired interval below zero says one model is better on 2026 balls like these; it doesn't say why, and with 3,583 death balls the death intervals are about three times wider than the all-ball ones.

7. Errors

The 29 worst-predicted death balls fall into four patterns, each written up as a hypothesis on the worst-predicted balls page. The match replay shows four whole death overs ball by ball.

  1. Big hitters at boundary-friendly grounds aren't expected to run twos or threes (10 balls).
  2. A new batter is expected to play himself in (6 sixes).
  3. The last balls of an innings are read as an all-out slog (10 balls: dots, singles and fours at 9 down).
  4. Wickets with no clear cause (3 balls).

Free hits: XGBoost gives a free-hit ball about a 5.9% chance of a wicket, which can't happen on a free hit except by a run-out. The logistic regression does better there.

8. Limitations

  • Free-hit wickets get about 5.9% from XGBoost, which isn't possible.
  • The data has no column for the type of extra, so wides and no-balls are told apart from the free hit that follows a no-ball.
  • Death-ball intervals are about three times wider than all-ball ones, because there are only 3,583 death balls.
  • The model barely separates death bowlers: swapping the bowler moves expected runs by only about 0.11 per ball.
  • The close-chase replay ends in a tie that KKR won in a super over; the super over isn't in the ball-by-ball data.
  • Player tables and the error analysis were made after the single scoring run, so they are descriptive.
Which limitations matter most for the conclusions.

9. Deviations from the proposal

  • Temperature scaling was promised but not adopted: XGBoost is used raw and the logistic regression uses vector scaling (see Methods).
  • Tuning and early stopping used 2024 rather than 2025. The frozen 2026 model early-stops on 2025 and is refit on 2008-2025.
  • The shrinkage strength was fixed at k = 100 rather than chosen on 2025.
  • No recent-form history features were built. Season features added nothing measurable.
  • 2026 was looked at again after the single scoring run (error analysis, player tables, post-freeze checks). Nothing in the models changed.
  • The replay matches were picked by a fixed rule after some per-match numbers had been seen. The rule is on the replay page.
  • The worst-balls page shows three lists instead of one: by XGBoost's loss (promised), by XGBoost's loss minus B1's, and the worst three of each outcome.
  • Player tables are extra and post hoc.
